SWING-A-BOX

A swing-out storage tray for a wheelchair user living with chronic joint pain and stiffness. It is light, opens with almost no effort, and mounts so it never blocks the chair. I led the design, the CAD and the magnetic detent, and printed every part on my own 3D printer.

The client

Designing for one person, not an average user

A client project in ENGINEER 1P13, McMaster's first-year engineering design course. Our client loves storytelling, writing and spending time with his dog. He uses a wheelchair, and chronic joint pain and stiffness make the zip bag he keeps his things in hard to open and unreliable while he's moving. Our job was to replace it.

Problem
Existing storage on the chair is unstable, which limits his independence and makes daily activities harder. He needs a reliable way to reach important items while mobile, with minimal effort. It must be lightweight, portable, durable, intuitive and built for repeated use, cost-effective to make at scale, and low-risk for the user.
Objective
Portable, lightweight and cost-effective, while staying sturdy under a steady load with minimal wobble.
Constraint 1
Safety first. Every edge filleted, no sharp points anywhere that could hurt him.
Constraint 2
It must never restrict his wheelchair movement or get in the way of the chair's other parts during use.
Function
Rotate outward on demand and act as an easy-to-use storage tray, with items held in place by a magnetic lid.
Design process

Sketch, cardboard, CAD, print

My concept was the one the team chose to develop. We took it through four rounds, changing something every week. Click through the stages.

Stage 1

My concept: a rotating tray

A rectangular tray on a swing arm, a magnetic detent so it rotates easily and holds its position, and a frame mount that stays clear of the armrest and controls. A silicone liner would absorb vibration and stop items sliding.

Selected by the team as the design to take forward.
Stage 2

Cardboard prototype

A quick physical mock-up to check the size of the tray, the length of the arm and how it swings, before committing to CAD.

Cheap to change: proportions were settled in cardboard, not in printed plastic.
Stage 3

CAD with a locking clasp

I modelled the tray, the swing arm and the detent hub with four magnet positions. This version clamped to the chair frame with a locking clasp and thumb screw.

Failed late: the clasp only fit one frame size, and the design had to work on any wheelchair he might use in future. See Changes.
Stage 4

Printed final prototype

I printed every part on my own 3D printer. The clasp became a Velcro strap that fits any armrest, and a magnetic lid replaced the zip, so it opens with almost no grip strength.

Added through iteration: magnetic lid and Velcro mount.
How it works

Swing it out, click, lift the lid

The tray rides on an arm that pivots at the detent hub. Magnets in the hub pull the arm into set positions, so it clicks into place and stays there, then swings away again with a light push. No clasps, zips or fine finger movements.

Magnetic detent

Magnets set in the hub give the arm fixed stops. It holds its position against bumps while moving, yet frees with a light push, with no latch to pinch.

Magnetic lid

Magnets at the corners keep the lid shut while the chair moves. Lifting it takes almost no grip, which matters for painful, stiff joints.

Velcro mount

A strap wraps the armrest, so the tray fits any chair he moves to, and it can be taken off without tools.

Filleted everywhere

Every edge and corner is rounded, so nothing he brushes against can cut or catch.

Printed and light

Thin-walled 3D-printed parts keep weight down and make each part cheap to reprint or change.

Low effort by design

Every action is a push, a swing or a lift. Nothing needs pinching, twisting or a firm grip.

What failed and what changed

Two late changes that made it better

1 · The clasp had to go

The team had finished its individual models and we were about to commit to the final design when we learned it had to work on any wheelchair he might switch to. Our locking clasp was sized for one frame, so a key component failed the brief at the last minute.

Rather than lose time brainstorming from scratch, we switched straight to our second-best option: Velcro. A clamp fits one tube diameter; a strap adapts to any armrest. The trade-off is a less rigid connection, so we watched wobble closely in testing.

2 · Testing moved it on top of the armrest

We planned to mount the system under the armrest. Running our testing plan showed it sat better on top, so I pushed for the change and we moved it.

Neither change was in the original plan. Both came from checking the design against the requirements every week, which is the main reason the final prototype works for him.

Reflection

A few highs, a lot of lows, and a better design

The idea on paper is never the final design. The first design is usually not the best one, and that is a good thing.

What this project taught me

What I learned

  • Persistence. A component failing at the last minute is a setback, not the end.
  • Change is good. Every iteration measured against the requirements made the design better.
  • Failure teaches. The clasp failing pushed us to a mount that works on any chair.

How I'll start the next project

  • With an open mind, ready for parts of the plan to fail.
  • Testing early against every requirement, so problems like the clasp show up sooner.
  • Treating each failure as information about what the design really needs.
