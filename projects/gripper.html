Q-Arm Warehouse Gripper

An end effector that lets a robotic arm pick customer orders in a mock warehouse. Our team designed, 3D printed and programmed a gear-synchronised claw. I was the project manager and designed the gears, the friction pads and the final jaw shape.

The brief

Pick a customer's order with a robotic arm

A team project in ENGINEER 1P13, McMaster's first-year engineering design course. The goal was an end effector for warehouse operations: a mechanism mounted on the Q-Arm that grabs items, identifies them and drops them into a basket to fill an order. That meant three phases brought together as one system: design, build and code.

Problem
Develop a mechanism that can grab and identify a variety of objects with maximum efficiency, while minimising material waste and physical strain.
Objective
A gripping claw that is lightweight and mobile, yet sturdy enough to lift objects of different weights, running alongside fully working code that completes the warehouse task.
Constraints
Minimise material waste and environmental impact. Keep cost and productivity efficient. Keep the workspace safe for every team member. Complete the assigned tasks quickly.
Function
Pick a selected number of items from a mixed set that differ in shape, size and mass, and move them to a basket to fulfil the user's order.
Design process

From five ideas to one claw

We started by sketching individual concepts, then merged the strongest features into one design. Click through the stages.

Stage 1

Combining our concepts

Each of us brought a design. The sketch merges them: two jaws driven through a gear pair by a single actuator, mounted on an effector platform, with a rubber surface and band at each fingertip.

Decision: one actuator drives both jaws through meshing gears, so the claw always closes symmetrically.
Stage 2

Full assembly in CAD

We modelled every part, checked the linkage moved freely, and prepared the files for 3D printing and laser cutting. The perforated platform keeps the part light and gives a grid of mounting points.

Built for: low material use, so only the parts that carry load are solid.
Stage 3

My contribution: the gear arms

I designed the gears. Each is a spur gear with the crank arm built into the same part, so there is no joint to slip between gear and arm. The two mesh directly: when the actuator turns one, the other turns the opposite way by the same amount.

Why it matters: the jaws mirror each other, so an object is centred as the claw closes.
Stage 4

Printed, assembled, padded

The printed parts are joined with screws and nuts at every pivot. I added friction at the fingertips with rubber bands and padding, and shaped the final notched jaw profile.

My addition: the friction pads. Bare plastic slips; rubber grips. The Grip section below shows by how much.
How it works

Two four-bar linkages, one gear pair

Each jaw is the moving link of a four-bar linkage: a crank on the gear, the jaw itself, and a short link back to a fixed pivot. Because the gears mesh, both cranks turn by equal and opposite amounts, so a single actuator closes both jaws in step.

Four-bar linkage

Four rigid links joined by four pivots. Turning one link (the crank) moves the others along a fixed path. Here the jaw is the coupler, so it both rotates and moves inward as it closes.

Meshing gears

Two equal spur gears in mesh turn in opposite directions at the same speed. That ties the two linkages together, so one actuator drives both jaws.

Why symmetric

If one jaw led the other, it would push the object sideways before the second jaw arrived. Mirrored motion meets the object from both sides at once and centres it.

Engineering check

Why the friction pads mattered

A claw holds an object by friction. Each jaw squeezes with a normal force N, and friction at both contacts has to carry the weight. Raise the friction coefficient μ and the same object needs far less squeeze, which means less load on the actuator and printed parts.

With bare print instead

Condition to hold: 2·μ·N ≥ SF·m·g, so N = SF·m·g / (2μ), with safety factor SF = 2. μ ≈ 0.3 for hard plastic and ≈ 0.7 for rubber are typical textbook values used to show the trend, not measured on our parts.

Takeaway. Moving from bare plastic to rubber more than halves the clamping force needed for the same object (0.3 → 0.7 is a 57% reduction). That is why a strip of rubber was worth more than a stronger actuator.
Software and integration

Individual functions, one main program

Each of us drew a flowchart and wrote functions for part of the Q-Arm's autonomous movement. We then combined them into a single main function that runs the whole order. Mine produces the order summary at the end.

Lesson. We left combining everyone's functions until the last days, which left little room for debugging. Next time I'd integrate early and often, so problems show up while there is still time to fix them.
Team and leadership

Project manager on a team of five

Work was split by interest and skill level. As project manager I kept the team organised, and I also worked across CAD, building, testing and code wherever help was needed.

The team

What we did together

  • Brainstormed individually, then merged our designs into one claw
  • Built the CAD model and prepared parts for 3D printing and laser cutting
  • Wrote flowcharts and functions for the Q-Arm's autonomous moves, then joined them into one main function
  • Ran test cases, collected data, and refined the mechanical and software parts after each round
Me

What I owned

  • Project management: ran weekly meetings outside Design Studio and set realistic weekly goals
  • Gears: designed the meshing gear arms that synchronise the jaws
  • Grip: added the friction pads at the fingertips
  • Jaw shape: set the final claw profile
  • Code: wrote the order-summary function

When the laser cutting fell through

Our laser-cutting request wasn't approved until it was far too late to use. Instead of panicking, I pushed the team to stay calm and focus on our next best action with what we could still make. It taught me that when plans change suddenly, a clear head finds the fix faster.

When deadlines slipped

Some delegated tasks fell behind because we weren't communicating clearly. I set weekly goals, realistic deadlines and regular check-ins so everyone knew what was due and when. A team only succeeds as one when it works as one.

Reflection

What I took away

For a team to succeed as one, it has to work together as one, and the foundation of that is healthy communication.

My first engineering project

Lessons I carry forward

  • Open-mindedness and communication matter as much as technical skill.
  • Stay calm under sudden change and look for the next best action.
  • A team's pace changes its results. I still wonder whether more time pressure would have made our meetings sharper, or whether more time would have bought better testing.

What I'd do differently

  • Combine everyone's code earlier, leaving real time for debugging.
  • Spend more time testing, so decisions rest on measured results rather than theory.
